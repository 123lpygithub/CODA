Perform constant comparison in this order:
1. Analytical-axis check: identify whether the research question asks about cause,
   location or construct, symptom, repair action, or another dimension. Treat candidates

2. Error-unit check: when several candidates come from one artifact, ask whether resolving
   one would leave another independently meaningful issue unresolved. Do not count changed
   lines, expressions, or locations as separate issues by default.
3. Case-to-case: compare the current evidence with relevant reviewed cases. Focus on similarities and analytically important differences.
4. Case-to-code: test whether each plausible active code's definition, inclusion criteria, and exclusion criteria explain the current evidence.
5. Code-to-code: only when the current case exposes overlap, synonymy, granularity, or category-boundary questions, compare the relevant active codes.
6. Synthesize coder agreement and disagreement after the comparisons. Agreement means
   compatible evidence-near readings, not agreement on codebook ids. Do not use majority voting.
7. Follow the selected task: case_comparison recommends reuse or records a refinement
   need without structural operations; codebook_refinement and batch_recheck may return
   justified add, add_parent, refine, merge, split, or rename proposals, or deferral.
   Do not invent unsupported category structure or treat a proposal as accepted.
8. Granularity check: treat coder candidates as evidence-near observations, not category
   definitions. Map observations with the same relevant meaning to a suitable reusable
   existing or proposed code, and preserve local manifestations in the comparison memo.
9. A new code must state an operational inclusion/exclusion boundary against the nearest
   existing code and ground that boundary in the current or supplied historical evidence.
   A merely imaginable future distinction is insufficient. If no grounded boundary can be
   stated, prefer reuse, refinement, a memo, or deferral rather than adding a narrow category.
10. Parent-abstraction check: whenever the comparison identifies at least two distinct
   active or proposed codes, directly ask whether they can be represented by one useful
   higher-level abstraction while remaining meaningful child categories. Recommend
   add_parent only when all of the following hold:
   - at least two child codes have distinct and defensible inclusion/exclusion boundaries;
   - observed artifacts support a shared semantic abstraction relevant to the research question;
   - the parent would help organize or compare future cases, rather than merely restating
     a generic domain label;
   - keeping the children under the parent preserves information that merge would erase.
   If the evidence is suggestive but insufficient, do not propose add_parent; preserve the
   children and create an open unresolved_question memo about the possible hierarchy.
11. Compact-induction decision: before recommending add or split, compare with the nearest
    existing category and explain why reuse or a coherent refinement would lose an observed
    distinction relevant to the research question. Put this reasoning in comparison_findings
    and the proposed change's rationale. If the new observation is another manifestation
    of the same concept, reuse the category and record the detail without creating a subtype.
    If relevant existing categories are redundant, propose their evidence-supported merge;
    adding an organizational parent alone does not reduce fragmentation.
